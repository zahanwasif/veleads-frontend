A good cold email reads like a short note from one person to another, written for that reader. It shows you know something specific about them, names a problem they probably have, and asks for something small. Everything else is noise.

This playbook covers the structure we use, a three-email sequence, and the mistakes we fix most often when we review a founder’s copy.

## What does a good cold email look like?

Four parts, in this order.

1. **A first line about them.** Something specific and true about their company or role.
2. **The problem.** One sentence on a problem that the specific thing suggests.
3. **Why you.** One sentence on how you solve it, with proof if you have it.
4. **A small ask.** A question they can answer in a few words.

Here’s what that looks like for a company that just started hiring SDRs.

```txt
Subject: ramping the new SDRs

Hi Sarah,

Saw Acme is hiring four SDRs this quarter. Congrats on the growth.

Most teams find the first two months go to building lists and warming
up inboxes before reps book a single call.

We set up the lists, domains and sequences in advance, so new reps
start with conversations instead of spreadsheets.

Worth a quick look at how that would work for your team?

Abdullah
```

It is under 80 words, has no links, and the ask needs one word to answer.

## How should you write the subject line?

Keep it short, plain and specific. Two to four lowercase words that sound like an internal email work well, because they don’t look like marketing.

- Good subjects name the topic, such as “ramping the new SDRs” or “question about outbound”.
- Avoid clickbait, fake replies like “Re:”, and anything with exclamation marks or emoji.
- Never promise something in the subject that the email doesn’t deliver.

## How do you write a first line that isn’t generic?

The first line decides whether the rest gets read. It has to be about them, and it has to be something you couldn’t paste into a hundred other emails.

| Weak first line | Strong first line |
| --- | --- |
| Hope you’re doing well | Saw you’re hiring four SDRs this quarter |
| I came across your company | Your post on churn in Q3 stuck with me |
| I love what you’re building | Noticed you just launched the EU version of the product |

The best first lines come from a real signal, such as a hiring post, a funding round, a product launch or something they wrote. That is also why picking accounts by signal makes copy easier.

## Why should the ask be so small?

Because the reader owes you nothing. Asking for a 30-minute call in the first email is asking a stranger for their time. Asking whether something is worth a look costs them one word.

Ask about interest, not availability. “Worth a look?” or “Is this on your radar?” gets more replies than “Do you have 30 minutes on Tuesday?”, and a yes leads naturally to booking a time.

## What should the follow-up emails say?

Many replies come from follow-ups, not the first email, so always send them. We send three emails to each prospect, spaced a few days apart.

| Email | When | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Day 1 | The full email, built on the four-part structure above |
| 2 | About 3 days later | A new angle or a short piece of proof, in two or three lines |
| 3 | About 4 days after that | A polite close, such as “Should I stop reaching out about this?” |

Send each follow-up as a reply in the same thread, so the reader sees the context. Never just write “bumping this up”. Each email should give them a new reason to answer.

## What are the most common copy mistakes?

- **Starting with yourself.** “We are a leading provider of” is about you. The reader cares about their problem.
- **Too long.** If it needs scrolling on a phone, cut it. Most of our first emails stay under 100 words.
- **Several asks.** One email, one question.
- **Links and attachments in the first email.** They hurt deliverability and add friction. Share them once someone replies.
- **Fake personalisation.** Inserting a company name into a template is not personal, and readers can tell.
- **No way to say no.** A line like “Not relevant? Just let me know” is polite and keeps people from hitting the spam button.

## How do you test and improve your copy?

Change one thing at a time, send each version to at least a few hundred similar prospects, and judge it on replies, not opens. Open tracking is unreliable and hurts deliverability, so we keep it off.

Read every reply, including the negative ones. They tell you which part of the message missed, whether it was the wrong person, the wrong problem or the wrong timing.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should a cold email be?

Short enough to read on a phone without scrolling. Most good cold emails land between 50 and 120 words.

### Should I use AI to write cold emails?

AI is useful for research and first drafts, especially for turning a signal into a first line. A person should still check every email, because generic or inaccurate AI copy is easy to spot and damages trust fast.

### Is it better to send plain text or HTML?

Plain text. It looks like a personal email, renders the same everywhere and is kinder to deliverability.

### How many follow-ups is too many?

Three emails in total is a good default. After that, move on and try a different channel or come back months later with a new reason to reach out.
